Why

A side project, made for fun. Copernicus publishes a model of the world’s air every day, free, as grids of numbers few people can read. Air Atlas is an experiment in turning them into something you look at.

It began as Who owns the air?, splitting each city’s pollution by sector. Those numbers were made up, so I dropped them and stayed with the model data. The live site kept the name.

Directions

Then several parameters at once. Each layer needed its own colour, and overlaps had to stay readable. I tried these in order:

  1. 8 Oct

    A plume over one city. Only its opacity was data; the shape was invented.

  2. 9 Oct

    A solid gradient shell, lifted higher where values were high.

  3. 9 Oct

    Several layers mixed into one shell.

  4. 9 Oct

    Dots of nearly fixed size, value as opacity.

  5. 10 Oct

    Halftone. Dot size is the value; each layer is its own offset plate.

Drawing rules

Size, not opacity.
Dot area matches the value.
Fixed plates.
Layers never move each other’s dots.
Quiet background.
Ozone shows only where it departs from its usual level.
Missing stays missing.
Gaps draw nothing. They are never zero.

Ink that mixes

Each pollutant is its own ink plate with a fixed offset. Overlaps mix the same way, whatever order you turn layers on.

Two models

Over Europe the atlas switches to an 11 km regional model. It doesn’t always agree with the 45 km global one: on the day I checked, its dust was about a quarter as high. The readout says which model you’re reading. Below: northern Algeria at close zoom. The small dots are the European model; the large ones, past its edge at 30° N, are the global one.

Edge of the European model

Data study

Correlations across all 405,900 cells. Fine particles track carbon monoxide where fires burn, and dust over the Sahara. A hint of smoke versus sand, not source attribution.
