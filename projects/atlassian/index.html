Context

In October 2020, Atlassian announced the end of support for its Server products and began moving customers to Cloud. A migration takes months.

The Cloud Migration Assistant is the tool admins use to plan, test and run it. I worked on its flows.

Problem

The brief was to build trust in the migration process. I broke it into questions, answered each one, and wrote the problem out again.

Trust

I read articles from NN Group and IxDF, research such as HBR’s The Neuroscience of Trust, talks by Simon Sinek and James Davis, and Atlassian’s own research and design docs. Three qualities kept coming up.

To explain them to the team, I used a story about learning to drive. Imagine a car with no indicators, steering that turns left when you turn right, and blacked-out windows. Would you feel confident driving it?

Users

Migrations are grouped by the customer’s size and how complex their setup is. I focused on complex enterprise customers.

Inside each customer, three kinds of admin take part.

Primary admin

Runs the software, and owns the tools used across the company.

Secondary admin

A technical expert who helps solve complex, specialised problems.

Satellite admin

Outside the IT team. Runs a separate instance that Atlassian has approved.

Pain points

I read reports and documents to find out why trust was low. Seven pain points came out of them. The most pressing, for the customers I focused on, was low confidence in the tooling.

Migration journey

A migration has six stages, spread over weeks or months. Admins use the assistant in four of them. The rest happens in Atlassian’s admin console, in meetings and in documents.

My three journeys sit where admins first open the assistant, and where they create, run and fix migration plans.

Three journeys

Inside the assistant, I worked on three journeys.

1

Creating a migration plan

1Creating a migration plan

Five steps: connect to a cloud site, choose the data, check it, review the plan and migrate.

Current flow

Admins create a plan from the assistant’s home: Migrate your data, then Create a migration. Five steps follow, from picking the cloud site to running the migration.

I went through each step as an admin and noted what got in the way of trust. Here is each step as it was.

Trust audit

Eighteen pain points in five steps. Choose had the most, so I scoped down to it.

Six pages

Choose was six pages: the migration options, then one page per kind of data, each laid out differently. Admins clicked back and forth to compare them.

Feedback

I took the audit to engineering, product management and data science. Each saw a different side of the problem.

Engineering

Some errors have to block a migration, or it fails later. A recommended plan was already being built.

Product management

Predicted times were often wrong, and failed plans cost trust. Complex migrations needed help from solution partners.

Data science

Earlier PURE and click-through studies showed which features admins left untouched. They helped me test my hypotheses.

Ideation

From there I scoped down to the Choose screen, and ran an ideation workshop with other designers. It showed where my flow depended on theirs.

Secret shopper
Use the product for the first time, through a test instance, and note everything that gets in the way.
PURE analysis
A group rates the mental load and effort each step of a task takes.

Final design

Two hypotheses shaped the final screen. The customer story behind both: as an admin, I want more context about each choice and its consequences while I pick what to migrate, so I can decide deliberately.

Condense the flow

A shorter, consistent flow means less friction, so trust grows.

Vertical tabs
Six pages become one. The tabs show the way through every section, and progress reads the same everywhere.

Add context

When admins see what each choice costs, they decide deliberately, fewer outcomes surprise them, and trust grows.

Selection summary
A collapsed summary for each section, the size and time of each, and the total time to migrate.

Some of my ideas, such as uploading a file to select projects, were already being built by other designers. The workshop caught that early.

2First impressions

The assistant’s home is an admin’s first look at the product. It listed tasks, but said little about the instance being moved.

Home

Three problems on the old home: little context about the instance, names that didn’t match their sections, and nothing on data access, security certification or privacy settings.

Show the data

If admins can see their instance, the system feels intuitive and transparent, and their confidence grows.

Map of the instance
Each part sized by its data. Hover for its size; click to open it.

Be open about security

If security and resources are out in the open, enterprise admins avoid their biggest blockers, and trust grows.

Resource monitor
Current use, to spot trends and decide on them.
Security information
Links to the documents admins need, where they need them.

3Changing a saved plan

Every migration starts as a plan: the cloud site, the data and the options. Plans are saved on the migrations dashboard. Admins come back to one when a run fails and they want to move what’s left, or when their strategy changes.

Saved plans

An example. The Q3 move had seven projects. Five migrated and two failed. The admin wants to retry the two, and add two projects that haven’t moved yet.

Problem

View details opened the plan at Review, near the end of the flow. To change it, the admin walked the flow backwards, then forwards again.

  1. 1

    Backwards

    The plan opened at Review. To change what moves, the admin stepped back through Check to Choose: the opposite of how the plan was made.

  2. 2

    Repeated steps

    After Choose, Check and Review ran a second time. Six steps for one change.

  3. 3

    No record of what moved

    Choose listed every project as if nothing had happened. The admin had to work out which five were done and untick them, or move them twice.

Fix

Hypothesis: a simpler flow makes the tooling consistent, so it’s easier to use, and admin confidence and trust grow.

1Backwards

Straight to Choose

Edit opens Choose with the saved plan filled in. The flow runs forwards, the way the plan was made.

2Repeated steps

Four steps

Choose, Check, Review and Migrate, each once. Two steps fewer.

3No record of what moved

Trimmed selection

A Migrated column shows what already moved, and those projects leave the selection on their own. One click restores them.

3Working out what’s left

Recommended plan

Suggests what to move next from the migration’s stage and earlier runs: here, the two failed projects and the two that haven’t moved.

What I learned

Documentation

Good documentation argues for a design when I’m not in the room, and keeps everyone talking.

Complexity

Complex environments take patience to understand. The learning doesn’t stop.

Challenges

Every problem needs its own understanding. Other disciplines see what I miss.

Outcome

I handed the designs over at the end of the internship. It was too early to measure how they did.
