Why bends

Plants move solids through pipes every day: ore and sand in mining, sludge in wastewater plants, catalyst powders in chemical reactors, pulp, grain and food. The bends are where trouble starts.

At a bend, fast water is thrown against the outer wall and wears it away, while slow water on the inner side lets particles settle. The turn also sets the water swirling sideways in two vortices, called Dean vortices, which decide where the particles go. We chose a bend to study them.

The thesis run at t = 2.00 s, cut open along the pipe’s mid-plane: blue is slow, red is fast. Drawn to scale from the dataset.

Problem

To see inside a pipe like this, engineers use computational fluid dynamics (CFD). The software splits the pipe into a mesh of tiny cells and solves the equations of fluid motion in every one, in small steps through time.

It is accurate, but slow, and every change to the setup means running it again. In the semester before this thesis I ran these simulations in OpenFOAM and waited on them. This thesis asks whether a model trained on the first part of a run can predict the rest, faster than the solver.

Case

A 40 mm pipe with a 90° bend, set up from OpenFOAM’s bentPipe example. Water enters at 0.5 m/s, about walking pace, carrying solid particles that make up 0.1% of its volume. It runs 10 diameters, turns upward and runs 10 more.

Drawn to scale from the mesh. Hover a sample plane to find it in the drawing.

Particles

The particles are split into 30 size classes from 1 to 250 µm, spaced evenly on a log scale. The solver tracks how much particle volume sits in each class as particles collide, stick together and break apart.

Circle sizes follow the log scale, not true size: the largest class is 250 times the width of the smallest. Shares are of the particle volume at the inlet, from the size distribution below.

Physics

The solver doesn’t follow each grain. It treats the particles as a second fluid mixed through the water, with its own speed at every point. Four forces pass momentum between the two.

Drag uses the Wen–Yu model and lift the Saffman–Mei model. Gravity also acts: the particles are 1.4 times as dense as water.

The equationsOptional
∂(αφρφ)∂t+∇·(αφρφuφ)=0

Mass. Nothing appears or disappears. For each phase φ: volume fraction α, density ρ, velocity u.

∂(αφρφuφ)∂t+∇·(αφρφuφuφ)−∇·(αφτφ)=−αφ∇p+αφρφg+Mφ+Sφ

Momentum. Newton’s second law for a fluid: stress τ, pressure p, gravity g, exchange M with the other phase, source S.

Mφ=∑ψ≠φ(FD+FL+FTD+FVM)

Exchange. The four forces above: drag, lift, turbulent dispersion and virtual mass.

Sticking and breaking

On the way through the pipe, particles bump into each other. Some stick and grow (aggregation). Strong eddies tear others apart (breakage). The solver tracks both, moving particle volume between the size classes.

How often each happens depends on particle size and on how turbulent the water is. The two charts show the rules the solver uses.

The equationsOptional
∂n(v)∂t+∇·(un(v))=Sv

Population balance. Counts particles of each volume v as they move, stick and break. Sv holds aggregation and breakage.

ad,d′=433π10εν(d+d′)3

Aggregation. Adachi et al. (1994). ε: how fast turbulence burns energy, ν: kinematic viscosity.

bv′=415πενexp⁡(−εcrε)

Breakage. Kusters (1991). εcr = B / rc, from particle strength B and collision radius rc.

Left: darker means two particles of those sizes stick more often, relative to two 1 µm particles. Large pairs stick millions of times more often. Right: below a critical turbulence level (εcr) particles barely break; above it, breakage climbs toward its limit.

Size distribution

Read it like a histogram: the higher a curve, the more particle volume sits at that size. At the inlet the peak is around 25 µm. One diameter after the bend it has moved to about 55 µm, and by nine diameters it is near 66 µm, as particles stick together on the way up the pipe.

Flow

Blue is slow and red is fast. The water starts at 0.5 m/s everywhere. Water touching a wall can’t move, so over the first second a slow layer grows from the walls, and the bend throws the fastest water toward its outer wall. From about 1.4 s on, the field barely changes.

Dean vortices

This swirl is why we picked a bend. In the bend, the fast water in the middle of the pipe is flung toward the outer wall, the way you lean outward in a turning car. The slow water near the walls can’t keep up, so it is pushed back around the sides toward the inner wall. The result is two counter-rotating swirls, named after W. R. Dean, who described them in 1927.

A stirred cup of tea does the same thing: the leaves gather in the middle of the bottom because a sideways circulation sweeps them there.

Model

From 41 CSV files to two XGBoost models.

How it works

From the solver’s output to a prediction, in six steps. Every number in this walkthrough comes from the thesis run.

Dataset

I exported the run from ParaView as one CSV per saved time step: 41 files, 651 MB in all. Each row is a mesh point with its position, the water’s velocity and the pressure. The files went to Kaggle for training.

Five rows from t1.55.csv, on the 1D-after plane. Positions in metres, velocity in m/s, pressure in Pa. The same files build every flow figure on this page.

Train and test

The model trains on every saved step up to 1.5 s and predicts the next ten, from 1.55 to 2.0 s. Most of the change in the flow happens before 1.4 s, so the test window is the flow settling into its final state.

Peak |U| on each sample plane at every saved step. The 1D-after plane speeds up as the jet along the outer wall forms. The 5D plane overshoots near 0.8 s before settling.

Features

One model per velocity component. To predict Ux at a point, the model gets Uy and Uz at that time and Ux at the three steps before. The water’s volume fraction can be added as an optional feature.

Real values in m/s from one mesh point on each sample plane, 7.4 mm off the symmetry plane. Each row is one training example.

XGBoost

XGBoost builds an ensemble of small decision trees. Each new tree fits what the trees before it got wrong, and its output is added with a small weight.

A gradient-boosted model fitted live in your browser to one real profile, 1D after the bend at t = 1.55 s: depth-2 trees, learning rate 0.3. The thesis models work the same way on many more features and points.

Next steps

What the thesis left for later.

Physics-informed models

Build the governing equations into the model, so predictions respect mass and momentum and hold up on new flow conditions.

Hybrid ML and CFD

Let the model explore a design space quickly, or give the solver a starting guess, and keep the solver for the final answer.

Real-time use

Fast predictions could feed flow control, monitoring and predictive maintenance in plants.

Other geometries

The same method applies to branching pipes, porous media and fluidised beds.

Uncertainty

Report how confident each prediction is, which matters where a wrong answer is costly.
