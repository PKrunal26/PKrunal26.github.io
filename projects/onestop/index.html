Problem

Every year about a thousand freshers walk through this gate into a campus they don’t know. Daily life ran on word of mouth, group chats and an inbox everyone shared.

Questions

Every year a new batch of freshers arrives and asks seniors the same questions. My batch spent its first year and a half online, so we arrived as lost as they were.

Answers lived in group chats, where a question scrolls away in minutes, and in the campus inbox, where everything from bus timetables to lost umbrellas goes to everyone. The mess menu was a hostel group’s profile picture.

Campus

Everyone lives on campus, on the north bank of the Brahmaputra across from the city. Hostels sit at one end, classes at the other. Ferries to the city are public transport, run outside the campus from three ghats along the river.

1.1 kmfrom the far hostels to the academic complex
3 ghatsfor ferries to the city, the nearest 2.1 km from the hostels
1–2 hbetween one bus or ferry to the city and the next
8,346people living on campus

Of them, 7,430 are students, with 406 faculty and 510 staff. Over a thousand students each year are first-year bachelor’s students, new to the place. They were the main audience.

Workarounds

I collected complaints from freshers and talked to juniors and peers. For each hurdle I noted how people got by. Every route ran through another person or an inbox.

Many freshers are introverts, and every workaround here means asking someone. That made each problem worse.

Word of mouth fails quietly. After a two-year break, everyone trained to run the campus broadcasting system had graduated, and nobody left could operate equipment worth over ₹10 crore.

Nine problems

Everything we heard fell into nine problems.

Travel

Ferry and bus timings are easy to miss. No directions to classes and buildings.

Food

No list of outlets with their menus, timings and locations.

Lost and found

No system, so lost items get mailed to everyone and bury important mail.

Timetable

Shared as a WhatsApp image, though not everyone in the group takes the same courses.

Mess menu

Hard to find, and timings change on weekends and holidays.

Complaints

No central portal, so complaints go unanswered.

Past papers

No access to previous year papers or seniors’ notes.

Selling

No way to buy or sell second-hand things, or even to know how.

E-rickshaw

No way to book one and no way to track it.

Decisions

One app, built by a student team that changes every year.

Constraints

Students had working habits already. Opening the app had to take less effort than the workaround, and the team had to be able to build and keep it.

A new team every year

Students graduate, and each year a new developer team takes over. The app had to be easy to hand on.

A wide scope

Travel, food, classes, lost items and more. Each part had to fit one system so later features could slot in.

Freshers on their way

The institute was calling freshers back to campus. We had to launch before they arrived.

Easy to use

Switching from a habit takes effort. Downloading and using the app has to cost less than the current system.

Easy to build

Every feature weighed against what the team could build with the skills it had.

Easy to grow

Not every feature could ship at once, so the design had to make room for the ones that came later.

Subapps

Building everything as one app risked chaos for users and for developers. We looked at apps that had the same problem, like Paytm, MyJio and M-Indicator, and split OneStop into subapps.

A shell holds login, home, search and navigation. Each subapp has its own small group of developers and can launch on its own. A later team can add one without touching the rest.

Home layout

I drew three home screens in lo-fi, then the whole team compared them and voted. The team was also the audience, which kept one person’s taste from deciding.

Timetable first

Tabs for each section, with the timetable open by default.

Icon grid

An icon for every service. Switching was quick, but the menu confused people and gave nothing to read at a glance.

Widgets Chosen

Widgets on top answer the common questions without a tap. Quick access to everything else sits underneath.

Structure

With the layout settled I wrote the information architecture so development could start. It doubled as the team’s checklist: we ticked sections off a whiteboard as they were built.

Design system

We needed a system the team could build fast, with nothing unnecessary in it. We adapted Google’s Material You: developers got ready-made components, and I designed the pieces campus life needed on top, like stop cards, class cards and outlet cards.

Accessible

    Bus, ferry or e-rickshaw. The next departure and the walk to the nearest stop.

  3. Next class

  4. Quick links

    LAN settings, past papers, a digital medical book and campus contacts.

  5. Services

    Lost and found, rent and sell, club blogs and intranet sites.

Lost and found became the most used feature. Rent and sell takes over each year as a batch gets ready to graduate. We later added cab sharing, to find people to split a cab to the airport or station.

Food

Food is mostly for first-years, who don’t know where to eat yet. Seniors use it to check whether their usual place is open.

Mess menu

The next meal for your hostel and day. Sign in once with your Outlook ID, and nobody zooms into the hostel group picture again.

Outlets

Most people don’t know where outlets are, so cards lead with distance, waiting time, closing time, and buttons to call or open the map.

Favourite dishes Later

Pick a dish and see every outlet selling it, best sellers first. It was hard to build, so it moved to a later release.

When I moved to the Coding Club I led the next version of this tab, Campus Cart, with in-app ordering, delivery and payment.

Travel

The next bus or ferry is often one to two hours away, so missing one costs an afternoon. Travel shows what leaves next and how long the walk to the stop takes.

Smart map

Directions to the nearest stop or ghat, or any stop you pick, with the walking time so you know if you’ll make it.

Live status

Each stop shows whether the bus has already left it, or when it will arrive.

E-rickshaws

They ran on phone calls. Tracking them needed the administration, and talks with them were ongoing.

Timetable

Students enter only their roll number. The app looks up their courses in the college directory and builds the timetable, with labs, sports courses and breaks. It follows the academic calendar for holidays and exams.

1 · Any student

Adds an assignment deadline or a quiz to a course.

2 · Class representative

Approves the request.

3 · Everyone in the course

Sees it on their timetable, and in Outlook.

In testing, people rarely looked more than five days ahead, since most timetables repeat every week.

Lost and found

Before OneStop, a lost item meant an email to everyone on campus. Now a found item is a short report, and the app keeps a record of where each item was handed in.

Quick links

Small things that every fresher asks about.

Contacts

Most students don’t have the medical section, emergency or Gymkhana numbers. Now they are a search away.

Internet settings

Many hostels have old LAN that needs a manual setup most people don’t know. The app walks through it.

Medical book

A digital copy of the paper medical book every student carries to the hospital, with history and past prescriptions.

Tested with friends and freshers, launched before the batch arrived, then fixed what users reported. The day got shorter.

What I learned

Research saves iteration

A problem found in user testing takes far longer to fix than the same problem found in research.

Argue for the design

Explaining my decisions to seniors was a skill I had to learn, and it kept the original vision intact.

Keep going

Technical problems delayed the launch more than once. Keeping the team’s spirits up got us there.

Get the app

OneStop IITG is on Google Play and the App Store. SWC teams have kept working on it since I graduated, so the live app may look different from these designs.
